## Societal context

Pip draws the benefits portal as one form and one decision. Residents add missing pieces: access to documents, caseworker practices, local institutions, and physical infrastructure. Societal context is a changing complex system around the software. Its agents include people and institutions, its precepts include their beliefs, and its artifacts include interfaces, datasets, laws, and infrastructure. The submission alone cannot explain the outcome.

## Agents, precepts, artifacts

The speech-coaching review begins with microphones. Pip asks who else belongs on the map. In SpeekSplendid, speech-impaired users and coaches are agents; beliefs about “good” speech and language norms are precepts; microphones and accessibility rules are artifacts. A tutoring example has learners, teachers, and lesson materials too. Naming the categories keeps an abstract model from erasing whose assumptions shape the service.

## Explicit causality

Pip expects appointment reminders to improve attendance. A resident asks, “Will the ferry arrive?” The assumed cause is now visible: a reminder may change behavior only when constraints permit it. Abstraction helps reasoning, but hidden assumptions distort it. Pip writes the causal relationship down so others can challenge it, including how changed access or behavior might alter data used in later decisions.

## Causal loops

Popular creators get more exposure, then more clicks, then a higher ranking. Pip draws the loop before treating clicks as independent proof of quality. Causal loop diagrams make reinforcing and balancing feedback discussable instead of showing only a one-way pipeline. The team can seek other perspectives, choose knowledgeable collaborators, and test assumptions. A loop suggests where an intervention might create another effect; it does not prove the prediction.
