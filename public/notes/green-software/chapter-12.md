# Chapter 12: The Green Software Maturity Matrix

*Pip’s adventure: Pip's badge needs evidence. Fictional teaching story; concepts follow the cited source.*

Building Green Software — Anne Currie, Sarah Hsu, and Sara Bergman. First edition, March 2024.

Source: printed pp. 181–192; text lines 6579–6981. References count literal newline-delimited lines in the supplied text.

## Overview

One cinema team automates rightsizing, and someone proposes an “Inspiring” badge for the whole organization. Pip asks what the other teams can demonstrate. The book’s Green Software Maturity Matrix describes five levels—Aspiring, Aware, Acting, Awesome, and Inspiring—across several axes. Training, inventories, and manual cleanup can begin the journey; repeatable processes, broader measurement, automation, and dynamic electricity response develop later. Operations, product management, device support, and education may advance unevenly. This is the supplied 2024 framework, not a current certification. Pip uses it to choose an evidenced next practice, not reward an ambitious label.

## Key ideas

### A maturity model guides improvement

Pip reaches for the highest badge, then opens the evidence folder. The maturity matrix describes practices and next steps, not an award earned by enthusiasm. Its five levels move from interest toward repeatable work and quantitative improvement, giving leaders and advocates a shared vocabulary. Pip brings inventory and recurring review records instead of an aspiration. The criteria belong to the book’s version; this learning exercise does not issue a certification. (Source: pp. 181–184.)

### Early action does not require perfect telemetry

The cinema wants perfect real-time telemetry before touching an idle environment. Pip proposes a reviewed inventory and cleanup while the baseline improves. Early practice can use retrospective data and qualified proxies to rightsize resources, choose suitable hosting, and build operational skills. The book encourages starting from actual capability. Elaborate measurement may help later, but it need not delay action on visible waste whose owners and recovery needs can already be checked. (Source: pp. 184–186.)

### Repeatability separates pilots from practice

Pip’s pilot clears a room of unused workers. Next month, another team recreates them. A successful project is not yet a repeatable practice. The Acting level emphasizes defined processes, broader emissions knowledge, recurring review, product waste, and device longevity; later levels add automation and quantitative response. Pip assigns owners, documents scope, schedules reviews, and records exceptions. The evidence must show the practice happens beyond one memorable cleanup. (Source: pp. 186–188.)

### Assess axes independently

The operations team celebrates repeatable rightsizing, but nobody owns older-phone support. Pip draws separate columns rather than averaging the gap away. The matrix covers commitments, footprint, metrics, operations, electricity, end-user devices, servers, product management, and education. Different owners may have different capabilities. Strong server practice cannot establish a missing device policy. Recording each axis’s evidence and uncertainty gives Pip a more useful next step than an unsupported overall score. (Source: pp. 188–191.)

### Distinguish annual claims from continuous response

A poster promises renewable electricity over a year. Pip asks for the time and place of actual operation. Higher maturity envisions real-time and forecast data, budgets, and use matched to carbon-free supply by interval and location. Annual purchases or future commitments do not demonstrate that capability. Pip records the claim under commitments and seeks operational evidence separately. The book’s matrix also focuses on greenhouse-gas-related software practice, not every environmental concern. (Source: pp. 181, 187–192.)

## Misconceptions

### One advanced team proves the whole organization is advanced.

Record the scope of evidence separately across teams and axes. A pilot does not establish a consistent practice.

### A maturity level is a measured emissions reduction.

A level describes practices and evidence. Actual resource and emissions outcomes need their own measurements.

## Decision practice — teaching extension

Pip reviews a cinema company with one rightsizing pilot, an annual emissions report, and keen volunteers. Other teams still provision manually; older-device support has no policy. Leaders need an honest next-quarter plan using the book’s matrix, not a badge based on the strongest team.

### Assess each axis and expand one repeatable practice

Makes gaps visible and turns a demonstrated technique into an achievable next step. Requires gathering evidence from several owners and accepting an uneven picture. The review records the platform pilot’s scope and assigns a broader operating process. Device-support work remains a separate gap with an owner.

### Invest first in shared real-time measurement

Could improve feedback for later quantitative decisions. Does not itself standardize operations or resolve unsupported devices. The team defines what decisions the new data would enable, while making clear that instrumentation is not proof of maturity on every axis.

The next investment should follow the bottleneck revealed by evidence. More detailed data can be useful without being the only path forward.

## Transfer to your work — teaching extension

Use the book’s framework to assess two areas you influence. Keep evidence, missing information, and aspirations separate. Choose one achievable next practice.

- Axes and current evidence: Which operations, metrics, device, product, or education practices can you demonstrate?
- Gaps and scope: Which teams or capabilities remain unverified or inconsistent?
- Next practice and review: Who will establish the next repeatable action, and what evidence will show it happened?
