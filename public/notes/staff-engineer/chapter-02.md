# Chapter 2: Three Maps

Original learning summary of Tanya Reilly's The Staff Engineer’s Path (2022). Source: text lines 1405–2580.

The three maps are a way to navigate a staff role: the locator map shows where your work sits, the topographic map shows organizational terrain and decision paths, and the treasure map keeps the destination and current position visible. Together they prevent local optimization from replacing useful perspective.

## Locator map

The locator map keeps the engineer oriented: what is important now, where the work sits, and what perspective is missing. Zoom out beyond your own team: which company goal does your project support, and how large is it in that wider landscape? This map counteracts the tendency to treat the nearest problem as the organization's most important problem.

## Topographic map

The topographic map records terrain such as reporting lines, informal power, decision forums, and cultural hazards. Formal reporting lines are only part of the terrain. Learn where decisions actually happen, who holds relevant knowledge, and which past experiences explain resistance. Comparing maps with colleagues exposes assumptions that an org chart cannot show.

## Treasure map

The treasure map connects a destination to the current position and the route, making progress and detours discussable. Identify a destination worth reaching, where the organization stands today, and the next part of the route. The map is a device for discussing direction, not a promise that every obstacle is already known or that the route cannot change.

## Local maximum

A local maximum is a solution that improves the nearby situation while making the wider objective harder to reach. A team can optimize its own delivery while increasing work for everyone downstream. Looking at the wider map reveals when a local improvement undermines the shared destination. The response may be coordination or a different objective, rather than harder local optimization.

## Teaching extension

The locator map says payments is the priority, the technology map shows a fragile identity dependency, and the topographic map reveals security approval as the real bottleneck. These invented practice choices are not reported incidents from the book. Use the reflection to apply the distinction to your own role.
