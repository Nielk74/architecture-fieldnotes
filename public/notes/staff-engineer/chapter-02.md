# Chapter 2: Three Maps

*Pip’s adventure: Three maps of the same workshop. Fictional teaching story; concepts follow the cited source.*

Original learning summary of Tanya Reilly's The Staff Engineer’s Path (2022). Source: text lines 1405–2580.

Pip finds three maps. The locator shows where the work fits; the topographic map reveals organizational terrain and decision paths; the treasure map connects today’s position to a shared destination. Comparing them keeps the workshop’s nearest problem from becoming its only priority.

## Locator map

The payment team waves Pip over: their problem feels like the whole workshop. Pip opens the locator map and zooms out. Which company goal does this project serve? How large is it in the wider landscape, and whose perspective is missing? Locating ownership helps, but orientation also means judging relative importance. The nearest problem is not automatically the organization’s most important one.

## Topographic map

The org chart gives Pip a route that never reaches approval. A colleague sketches another: informal influence, the actual decision forum, and a security expert absent from the chart. This is the topographic map. Past experiences and cultural hazards help explain resistance. Pip compares maps with others, learning who holds knowledge and where decisions happen rather than mistaking reporting lines for the whole terrain.

## Treasure map

Pip draws a flag over “safe independent releases.” The treasure map needs more than a destination: where is the workshop now, and what is the next part of the route? Colleagues mark obstacles and discuss a detour. The map makes direction and progress discussable. It is not a promise that every hazard is known or that reaching the flag will follow the first line drawn.

## Local maximum

A local cache makes one team faster but blocks the shared reliability plan. Pip steps back from the glowing team metric. A local maximum improves the nearby situation while making the wider destination harder to reach; downstream colleagues may carry the extra work. The answer may be coordination or a different objective, not harder optimization. Pip asks what improves the workshop’s outcome, not just this corner.

## Teaching extension

Pip’s locator map prioritizes payments. An identity dependency looks fragile; the topographic map reveals security approval as the bottleneck. The owners see different routes. These invented practice choices are not reported incidents from the book. Use the reflection to apply the distinction to your own role.
