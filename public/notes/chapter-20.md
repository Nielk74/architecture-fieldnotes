# Chapter 20: Analyzing Architecture Risk

Risk is part of every architecture, whether the concern is availability, scalability, security, or data integrity. The chapter offers a repeatable path from uncertainty to action. Its risk matrix rates impact and likelihood independently from one to three, then multiplies them. Scores one and two are low, three and four medium, and six through nine high. The source explicitly says to assess impact first and likelihood second. A high-impact event on a highly available cluster may therefore be medium overall when its likelihood is low (source lines 10139–10216; pp. 297–298).

A risk assessment aggregates these ratings by meaningful criteria and by service or domain area. Totals show where risk accumulates, but a meeting needs signal rather than every row. Filter the report to the relevant dimension or high-risk items. A snapshot also lacks direction. Plus and minus signs or arrows toward a target score can show whether risk is improving or worsening, as long as the meaning remains clear without a distant key. Continuous measurements and fitness functions can provide evidence for trends (lines 10217–10343; pp. 298–301).

Risk storming adds perspectives that one architect cannot provide alone. The exercise should include architects and, where possible, senior developers and tech leads. Participants first work independently so early opinions do not anchor the group. They mark risk areas on an up-to-date architecture diagram using the matrix. The collaborative portion then reaches consensus, investigates disagreement, and identifies unfamiliar technologies. An unknown technology receives the highest rating until the team understands it; a disagreement may reveal experience or a knowledge gap rather than a mathematical error (lines 10344–10504; pp. 301–306).

The three stages are identification, consensus, and mitigation. Identification can be prepared one or two days before the session and should focus on one dimension when possible. Consensus consolidates the notes and asks why a participant saw a risk others missed. Mitigation changes the architecture or adds safeguards, such as a queue for back pressure, a separate database for a critical capability, or a cache. These changes cost money and effort, so stakeholders decide whether the reduction outweighs the expense. The book's database example shows how a less expensive split can be accepted when full clustering and federation are rejected (lines 10505–10589; pp. 306–307).

The nurse diagnostics example demonstrates successive risk dimensions. A central database threatens availability because call routing depends on it; the diagnostic provider is risky because its capacity and availability constrain the system. Separate profile and case-note databases, plus researched availability agreements, address part of the concern. For elasticity, queues add back pressure and priority, while cached outbreak questions avoid the provider bottleneck. For security, separate gateways prevent self-service or administrative paths from reaching medical records. These are changes driven by collaborative findings, not decorations added after the fact (lines 10590–10704; pp. 308–314).

Risk storming is continuous: repeat it after major features, refactoring, or iterations. The same matrix can rank the impact and likelihood of failing to complete an Agile story. Teaching extension: the workshop scenario and its option labels are invented. They invite prioritization and negotiation without claiming measured risk for a real health system. A useful artifact records the risk dimension, score, evidence, mitigation cost, residual risk, and trend.
