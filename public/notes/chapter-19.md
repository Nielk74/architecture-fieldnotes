# Chapter 19: Architecture Decisions

Architecture decisions guide development toward a structure and a set of characteristics that serve the system. They require enough information, a reasoned choice, documentation, and communication to affected stakeholders. The chapter organizes common failures as a progression. Covering Your Assets avoids a decision from fear of being wrong. The remedy is the last responsible moment: wait for enough information to justify the choice, but decide before progress is blocked, while collaborating with the developers who will expose implementation problems. Groundhog Day occurs when a decision's why is absent and the same argument returns. Email-Driven Architecture follows when a decision exists only in transient messages rather than one durable record (source lines 9589–9738; pp. 281–283).

The architecturally significant boundary is broader than “architecture diagrams.” A decision matters architecturally when it changes structure, important nonfunctional characteristics, dependencies, interfaces, or construction techniques. A technology choice belongs in this category when it affects performance, scalability, security, or another characteristic. This test helps an architect document the choices that constrain other teams and future change without turning every coding preference into an ADR (lines 9739–9834; pp. 283–284).

An Architecture Decision Record is a short, focused text document, commonly one or two pages. Its core sections are Title, Status, Context, Decision, and Consequences. Compliance and Notes are useful additions. Status may be Proposed, Accepted, or Superseded; a time-boxed Request for Comments status can gather feedback without permitting discussion to continue forever. Links between a superseded ADR and the record that replaces it preserve the history of what changed and why. Approval rules should be explicit, especially for cost, cross-team impact, or security (lines 9835–9943; pp. 285–287).

Context states the forces that make a decision necessary and can identify the alternatives. Decision should speak affirmatively and explain why, because readers can often recover how an implementation works but cannot recover why it was chosen. Consequences make benefits, costs, and architecture-characteristic trade-offs visible. The source's examples show why this matters: changing a low-latency RPC to a more decoupled mechanism may create timeouts if the original performance rationale is forgotten. Recording the trade-off prevents a later refactor from repeating the old mistake (lines 9944–10042; pp. 287–289).

Compliance asks how the decision will be governed. A manual review may be enough, but an automated fitness function can check a rule such as where shared services reside. Notes preserve authorship, approval, modification, and supersession metadata. ADRs should be stored where affected people can access them, with one record per decision and a consistent structure for application, integration, and enterprise concerns. The chapter recommends a wiki or shared document structure for broad access, while acknowledging that a source repository can work for decisions local to that repository (lines 10043–10100; pp. 289–292).

ADRs can also explain standards. The context establishes the problem, the decision states the rule and its reason, and consequences expose whether the rule is worth enforcing. If the architect cannot justify a standard, its existence deserves reconsideration. Teaching extension: the order/payment scenario is invented as practice. Its REST and messaging options illustrate how a learner can record responsiveness, burst handling, reliability, and error-management costs; the numbers and outcome are not source measurements. Draft a compact ADR with a status and one measurable compliance check.
